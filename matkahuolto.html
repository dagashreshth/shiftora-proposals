Forecasting what has not happened yet

the pattern most relevant to Matkahuolto
Demand forecasting on long lead times
Listed manufacturer · India · in production

The problem. A listed manufacturer committing to supply against long lead times on spreadsheets and instinct. Every forecast miss became dead stock or a stockout weeks later, and nobody could say which lines to trust.

What we built. Forecasting across the full range, decomposed for seasonality and promotion before any model saw it, with commitment signals per line and a rolling backtest that re-proves the number every cycle.

Why it worked
  • Tested only on periods it had never seen. The most recent year was sealed before any modelling began, so the number is out-of-sample rather than fitted.
  • Bias measured separately. Accuracy alone can hide systematic over-commitment, so the two are gated together.
  • The incumbent method was scored first. The claim is the difference, on the same data and the same window.

What this means in practice

  • Capacity is known before departure, not after. How much space a service will actually have, predicted early enough to sell it or fill it.
  • Allocation stops depending on who is on shift. The same parcel gets the same decision at eleven at night as at nine in the morning.
  • Promises are checked before they are made. A service commitment the plan cannot keep is caught at lodgement rather than discovered at the depot.
  • Every departure teaches the next one. What actually fitted, and what did not, becomes the forecast for the same service next week.

Why Matkahuolto, and why now

  • The asset cannot be copied. A competitor can buy vans. Nobody else in Finland can put a parcel on a service that was departing anyway.
  • The constraint is fixed, which makes prediction more valuable. You cannot add a departure to solve a problem, so knowing about it early is the only lever.
  • Nothing is replaced. Your systems, your timetables and your operator arrangements stay exactly as they are.
  • Your history is the asset. Every parcel that has ever travelled is a labelled example of what fits, when. They are simply not being learned from.

03

What we see at Matkahuolto

Four observations, drawn from public sources and from patterns common to scheduled networks carrying freight. We have seen no internal data, so treat these as hypotheses to correct. Each names the business consequence, not just the symptom.

Observation 01 · capacity that expires

Space on a departing service is worth nothing the moment it leaves.

A scheduled network produces capacity whether or not anything is loaded into it. That is the model, and it is why the marginal parcel is so much cheaper to move than for any competitor.

But unlike warehouse space, it cannot be held. Every service that leaves with room is revenue that existed for a few hours and then did not.

Observation 02 · capacity you orchestrate

You coordinate vehicles you do not own.

The network is assembled from many independent operators, each with their own agreements, limits and handling conditions. Holding that together is a genuine operating achievement.

It also means every allocation decision has to respect arrangements that live in people rather than in a system, so the decision cannot be delegated and cannot easily be checked.

Observation 03 · a different problem

A fixed timetable is a harder problem than a flexible fleet.

Route optimisation assumes the vehicle can go somewhere else. Here it cannot. The timetable is the constraint, and freight has to be fitted to it rather than the other way round.

That makes conventional routing tools the wrong shape. The valuable question is not which way to drive but what should be on which departure, and how much room it will really have.

Observation 04 · three businesses, one network

The same movement serves passengers, parcels and operators.

Passenger services, parcel services and carrier services all draw on the same physical departures, which is what makes the economics work at all.

It also means a decision that is good for one can quietly cost another, and no single person sees all three at the moment the decision is made.

Why this matters commercially

None of this is a failure. Running freight on a passenger network is genuinely difficult and nobody else in the country has managed it at this scale.

The opportunity is that allocation is a decision made continuously, against a constraint that never moves, using data you already hold. Prediction is worth more here than almost anywhere, precisely because you cannot add a departure to fix a mistake.

We would not go near your timetables, your operator relationships or your commercial terms. Those are your team’s ground.

Where we would start

One recommendation, not a menu. Predict how much room each departure will really have and propose what should travel on it, so your team confirms a plan rather than assembles one. No change to timetables. No change to operator arrangements. The system behind it already runs in production. Prove it on one corridor, then let the numbers decide the rest.

Capacity forecast and allocation, on one corridor
the recommended first build

The system forecasts the space each scheduled departure will actually have, matches lodged parcels against it under each operator’s own conditions, and proposes a plan with one line of reasoning behind each placement.

Your team confirms or changes it. Nothing is committed to a departure and no promise reaches a customer without a person.

Fuller departures are the obvious gain. They are not the largest one. Knowing early that a service will be short is what turns a missed promise into a different plan, and on a fixed timetable early warning is the only lever you have.

How the rollout runs
  • Discovery. One corridor, one service type. We map how allocation is decided today and agree the single number we will be judged on.
  • Design. Operator agreements and handling conditions become settings your team can edit, not code only we understand.
  • Integration. Read-only against your parcel history and timetable data. No migration. Nothing changes for operators or customers.
  • Deployment. Two weeks running quietly alongside the live process, so the baseline is measured rather than guessed. Then live, with your team confirming every plan.
  • Continuous improvement. Results reported weekly. Every departure improves the next capacity forecast.

How we work

  • One number per build, agreed in writing before we start and evaluated on a date fixed in advance.

What we will not do

  • Not change your timetables. Services run as scheduled. This fits freight to the network rather than the reverse.
  • Not touch operator relationships. Agreements, limits and handling conditions are yours. The system applies them, it does not negotiate them.
