03

What we see at Trans-Pro Logistics

Four observations, drawn from public sources and from patterns common to non-asset brokerages. We have seen no internal data, so treat these as hypotheses to correct. Each names the business consequence, not just the symptom.

Observation 01 · the front door

The quote button starts a person, not a system.

A rate request form is the entrance to the business. What happens behind it is a broker reading the request, recalling the lane and deciding a number.

So the speed of that reply is not an operational detail. In brokerage the first credible number very often wins the load, which makes reply speed a revenue number wearing an operations costume.

Observation 02 · the asset

Your network answers phones, not APIs.

A carrier base built on small fleets and owner-operators is a real advantage on price and flexibility, and it is not something a large asset carrier can copy.

It is also why covering a load is phone work. Who runs a lane, who accepted what, and who performed sits with the broker who made the call, which means the most valuable thing the business owns leaves when they do.

Observation 03 · the border

A crossing is a second job on the same load.

Moving between Canada, the United States and Mexico means each load carries a documentation step alongside the freight. The truck is one problem. What lets it cross is another.

A cross-border load therefore costs more operational time than a domestic one of the same value, and today that difference is absorbed by people rather than priced into anything.

Observation 04 · the arithmetic

In brokerage, a manual minute is a margin point.

A brokerage earns the difference between what a shipper pays and what a carrier accepts. That spread is fixed the moment the load is booked.

Everything after it, the sourcing calls, the status emails, the chasing of a document, is cost charged against a number that can no longer move. Volume does not fix this. It multiplies it.

Why this matters commercially

None of this is a failure. It is how almost every brokerage is built, because the people are genuinely good at the work and the relationships are real.

The opportunity is that a brokerage is unusually exposed to its own manual minutes. The margin on a load is fixed the moment it is booked, so every hour spent quoting, sourcing and chasing afterwards is subtracted from a number that can no longer move. This is the rare case where removing work and improving margin are the same sentence.

We would not go near your pricing judgement, your carrier relationships or your customers. Those are your team’s ground.

Where we would start

One recommendation, not a menu. Read every rate request on arrival, answer it with a draft priced from what the lane has actually cleared at, and rank the carriers who run it before anyone picks up a phone. No change to how shippers reach you or how you deal with carriers. No platform migration. The system behind it already runs in production. Prove it on one lane, then let the numbers decide the rest.

Quoted and shortlisted on arrival
the recommended first build

A rate request is read on arrival, in whatever channel it comes in. The system pulls what that lane has actually cleared at, applies your costing and border steps, and puts a draft quote and a ranked carrier shortlist in front of your broker.

Your broker sets the price and chooses the carrier. Nothing is quoted or covered on the system’s word.

Answering faster is the obvious gain. It is not the largest one. Every quote and every cover becomes a record, so lane knowledge stops living in one person’s memory and starts belonging to the business.

How the rollout runs
  • Discovery. One lane. We map how a quote is produced today, from request to sent, and agree the single number we will be judged on.
  • Design. Your costing, border steps and margin rules become settings your team can edit, not code only we understand.
  • Integration. Read-only against the mailbox, the quote form and the TMS you already use. No migration. Nothing changes for shippers or carriers.
  • Deployment. Two weeks running quietly alongside the live process, so the baseline is measured rather than guessed. Then live, with your brokers pricing every quote.
  • Continuous improvement. Results reported weekly. Every quote won or lost, and every cover that held or failed, improves the next one.
