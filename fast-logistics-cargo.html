What we run in production

Two systems matter for this conversation. One hunts rates across suppliers and answers customers without a person starting the work. The other is the supervised loop that makes those decisions safe to trust at volume. Both are shown here as they run, before anything is proposed.

Quoting and answering, automatically

the pattern most relevant to Fast Logistics
Rate search and customer response, run by agents
Musafir.com · UAE · in production

The problem. A leading UAE travel enterprise with a high-volume booking operation. Every fare had to be hunted across multiple suppliers by hand, and every customer question waited for a person to pick it up. Response speed was a function of who was on shift.

What we built. The whole platform in seven weeks, and the agent layer around it: a fare-search agent that finds the cheapest viable option across suppliers, tracking on every order, automatic customer notification at each stage, and a support assistant that repairs its own broken conversations. Failed steps retry, re-plan, or escalate to a person.

Why it worked
  • Search became continuous. Options are hunted across suppliers automatically rather than when someone has a free moment.
  • The customer stopped asking. Status is pushed at every stage, so the enquiry never arrives in the first place.
  • Nothing consequential is automatic. The agent prepares; a person approves anything that commits the business.

Making it safe to trust

the discipline behind every system we ship

What we built. A supervised loop. The system decides and scores its own confidence. Anything uncertain goes to a person. Every correction becomes training data, so accuracy improves in production instead of being trusted at launch.

In plain terms

  • An enquiry stops waiting for a free moment. It is read, priced and drafted on arrival, whatever hour it lands in and whichever office it lands in.
  • Your team prices, rather than gathers. The hours spent assembling a quote become minutes spent deciding what to charge for it.
  • The status question stops arriving. Milestones are pushed to the customer, so the call that interrupts an operator never gets made.
  • Every quote becomes evidence. Won or lost, at what price, on which lane. Today that history exists only in inboxes.

Why Fast Logistics, and why now

  • Speed is the brand. The operational speed is real: six weekly freighters and licensed customs brokerage. The commercial response is where it is not yet matched.
  • Sixty offices multiply the same task. The identical enquiry is worked separately in each. That is exactly where one shared system pays for itself.
  • Nothing is replaced. There is no platform to migrate off, so this sits behind the inboxes and processes your team already uses.
  • The history is the asset. Years of quotes and shipments already hold the pricing intelligence. It just is not queryable yet.

03

What we see at Fast Logistics

Four observations from what Fast Logistics publishes, and from patterns common to forwarders growing across many offices. We have seen no internal data, so treat these as hypotheses to correct rather than findings. Each names the business consequence we would expect, not just the operational symptom.

Observation 01 · the front door

Every enquiry starts as an email.

The website offers services, network and contact details. It offers no rate request, no quotation tool and no shipment tracking; customers are directed to a contact form and an info address. The consequence is that response time is a staffing decision rather than a system property, and in freight the first credible quote usually wins.

Observation 02 · the promise

The operations are fast. The reply is not.

Six weekly freighters, licensed customs brokerage, dangerous goods capability and 200,000 sq ft of UAE warehousing are genuine operational speed. The consequence is a gap between what the company can do and what a prospect experiences first, and buyers form their judgement on the second.

Observation 03 · the network

Sixty offices, sixty inboxes.

A presence across 14 countries means the same enquiry type is worked separately in each location, priced from local memory. The consequence is inconsistent pricing on comparable lanes, no pooled view of what is being asked for, and pricing intelligence that leaves when a person does.

Observation 04 · the cost curve

Growth is being bought with headcount.

When quoting and status handling are manual, every increment of volume needs an increment of people. The consequence is that margin per shipment stays flat or falls as the business grows, which is the opposite of what scale is supposed to do.

Why this matters commercially

None of this is a failure. It is how almost every successful forwarder is built, because the people are genuinely good at the work. The opportunity is that quoting and status handling are the two highest-volume, most repeatable tasks in the business, which makes them the cheapest to change and the fastest to show a result. And we would not go near your pricing judgement, your carrier relationships or your customer relationships. Those are your team’s ground.

04

Where we would start

One recommendation, not a menu. Automate the path from enquiry to draft quote, so your team prices instead of gathers. It needs no change to how customers contact you, no platform migration, and the system behind it is running in a UAE enterprise today. Prove it on one office and one trade lane, then let the numbers decide the rest.

Enquiry to draft quote, on one lane
the recommended first build

The system reads enquiries as they arrive in whatever form they come, extracts lane, mode, cargo and dates, searches carrier tariffs, agent quotes and your own quoting history, applies your surcharge and margin rules, and prepares a draft in your format with every line traceable to its source. Your team sets the price and sends it. Nothing quotes itself and nothing reaches a customer without a person.

Speed is the obvious gain. It is not the largest one. Every quote won or lost becomes evidence of what wins on which lane at what price, and that history is currently trapped in inboxes.

How the rollout runs
  • Discovery. One office, one trade lane. We map how a quote is produced today and agree the single number the build will be judged against.
  • Design. Your surcharge, handling and margin rules become configuration your team can edit, not code only we can change.
  • Integration. Read-only against the mailbox and rate sources you already use. No migration, no change for customers.
  • Deployment. Two weeks drafting in shadow beside the live process, so turnaround is measured rather than estimated. Then live, with your team pricing and sending every quote.
  • Continuous improvement. Accuracy reported weekly. Every quote won or lost trains the next one.

How we work

  • One number per build, agreed in writing before we start and evaluated on a date fixed in advance.
  • Weekly reporting in a consistent format, to your team, whether the news is good or not.
  • People decide. The system drafts. Your team prices, approves and sends.
  • Your environment. Data stays in Fast Logistics’ systems and jurisdiction. We integrate rather than extract.
  • We stay. Monitoring, rule updates, retraining and support continue for the life of the system.

What we will not do

  • Not change how customers reach you. The same inbox, the same numbers, the same people. This sits behind them.
  • Not set your prices. The system assembles cost and evidence. Margin is a commercial judgement and stays yours.
  • Not send anything automatically. No quote and no commitment reaches a customer without a person approving it.
  • Not expose you to other clients. Isolation is audited rather than assumed. Your rates and customers stay yours.
  • Not create dependency. Your team is trained to run what we deliver. That it can operate without us is a design requirement.
