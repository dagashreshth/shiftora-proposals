LegalMatch.com · USA · in production

The problem. A US marketplace approaching $100M revenue with far more inbound demand than its sales floor could work. Effort was spread evenly across every enquiry, so the best opportunities got the same attention as the worst and nobody could defend the order of work.

What we built. Three calibrated probability models, one per funnel stage, inside the system the team already used. Every opportunity ranked by expected value, and every ranking explainable to the person acting on it.

Why it worked
  • Calibrated, not just ordered. When the model says 60 percent, roughly 60 in 100 happen. That makes the score usable for planning, not only for sorting.
  • Explainable at the row level. The person sees why something ranks where it does. Ranking without reasons gets ignored however accurate it is.
  • Corrections feed back. Every human override becomes training data, so the model tracks reality rather than the month it was built.

03

What we see at Supersourcing

Four observations, drawn from public sources and from patterns common to two-sided marketplaces matching people to work. We have seen no internal data, so treat these as hypotheses to correct. Each names the business consequence, not just the symptom.

Observation 01 · what a match is

A match is not a ranking. It is an agreement.

Ranking engineers against a brief is one side of the problem. The engineer or the agency has to want it too: the rate, the stack, the duration, the timezone, the client. A perfect technical fit that declines is not a match.

So what decides your match rate is not how well you can rank supply. It is whether both sides say yes, and today that is discovered by making the introduction and waiting to see.

Observation 02 · the inventory

Unmatched supply walks away, and it does not tell you.

An engineer waiting on a match takes another project. A client waiting on a shortlist is already talking to two other platforms. Neither of them writes to say they have gone.

So every day a match takes is a day either side of it may quietly disappear. That makes time to a credible shortlist the number a marketplace actually lives on, and it is currently produced by people reading profiles.

Observation 03 · where your AI points

Your AI is aimed at finding. The saying yes is still discovered.

AI-driven sourcing and profile screening are published capabilities, and your technical team evaluates on top of them. That is the right order: find well, then judge well.

What neither of those predicts is whether the engineer will accept. A profile can be the best technical answer to a brief and still say no, and today that only becomes visible after somebody has made the introduction.

Observation 04 · the expensive failure

The match that breaks after it starts costs the most.

A placement that ends early costs the client a delayed project, the engineer a gap, and you the fee, the replacement and some of the reputation that brought them both.

The signals that predict it are already in your own history: which pairings lasted, on which stacks, at which rates, with which clients. Today that is an outcome you record rather than evidence you use.

Why this matters commercially

None of this is a failure. Building enough vetted supply that almost any brief has a real answer is the hard part of a marketplace, and most attempts never get there.

The opportunity is that you are now on the other side of that problem. With supply this deep, the constraint stops being whether a good engineer exists for a brief and becomes which of the many is worth introducing. That is a ranking problem, you already hold both sides of it, and every past introduction is a labelled example of the answer.

We would not go near your technical evaluation, your client relationships or your judgement about people. Those are your team’s ground.

Where we would start

One recommendation, not a menu. Score every brief against every available engineer on two things separately, how well the skills answer it and how likely that engineer is to accept, and hand your team a shortlist with the reasoning attached before anyone makes an introduction. No change to your marketplace, your vetting or how either side reaches you. No platform migration. This exact build already runs in production for a marketplace with the same problem. Prove it on one stack, then let the numbers decide the rest.

Both sides, before the introduction
the recommended first build

Every brief is read against every available engineer, and every engineer against every open brief. The system scores two things separately: how well the skills answer what was asked for, and how likely this engineer is to accept, based on your own record of who has said yes to what, at what rate, on which stack.

Your team makes the introduction. Nothing is proposed to a client or an engineer without a person.

Building the shortlist faster is the obvious gain. It is not the largest one. A shortlist that predicts acceptance is a shortlist that converts, and in a marketplace the match rate is the whole business.

How the rollout runs
  • Discovery. One stack, one client segment. We map how a shortlist is built today and agree the single number we will be judged on.
  • Design. What counts as a strong fit, and how much acceptance is weighted against it, become settings your team can edit rather than code only we understand.
  • Integration. Read-only against the marketplace and the systems your team already works in. No migration. Nothing changes for a client or an engineer.
  • Deployment. Two weeks running quietly alongside the live process, so the baseline is measured rather than guessed. Then live, with your team making every introduction.
  • Continuous improvement. Results reported weekly. Every acceptance, every decline and every placement that lasted improves the next score.

How we work

  • One number per build, agreed in writing before we start and evaluated on a date fixed in advance.
  • Weekly reporting in a consistent format, to your team, whether the news is good or not.
  • People decide. The system reads, scores and explains. Your team decides who is introduced to whom.
  • Your environment. Data stays in Supersourcing’ systems and jurisdiction. We integrate rather than extract.
  • We stay. Monitoring, rule updates, retraining and support continue for the life of the system.

What we will not do

  • Not change how either side reaches you. The same marketplace, the same account managers, the same people. This sits behind them.
  • Not replace your technical evaluation. Judging whether an engineer is genuinely good is what your team is for, and it is the reason clients trust the shortlist. This decides who reaches them, not who passes.
  • Not introduce anyone automatically. No engineer is put in front of a client, and no project in front of an engineer, without a person deciding it.
  • Not expose you to other clients. Isolation is audited rather than assumed. Your engineers, your clients, your rates and your match history stay yours.
  • Not create dependency. Your team is trained to run what we deliver. That it can operate without us is a design requirement.
